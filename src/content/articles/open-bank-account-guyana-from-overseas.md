Whether you're building a house, collecting rent from a property in Georgetown, preparing for [remigration](/articles/guyana-remigrant-scheme-guide/), or just tired of sending pickup transfers, at some point many overseas Guyanese want an actual bank account back home. Here's the realistic picture of getting one from abroad.

## The banks

Guyana's retail banks include **Republic Bank**, **Guyana Bank for Trade and Industry (GBTI)**, **Demerara Bank**, **Citizens Bank**, and **Bank of Baroda**, plus the New Building Society for savings and mortgages. All offer savings and chequing accounts; most offer **foreign currency accounts** (US dollars in particular) alongside Guyanese dollar accounts.

There's no strong "best bank for the diaspora" answer — the practical differentiators are the branch near your family or property, the state of their online banking, and how their compliance team treats non-resident applicants. That last one varies more than the brochures admit.

## The honest headline: expect to show up

Guyanese banks operate under strict **anti-money-laundering and know-your-customer rules**, and the correspondent banking relationships they depend on make them conservative with overseas customers. In practice:

- **Most banks want you in the branch** to open the account — signatures, originals, photos
- Some accommodate opening via a **power of attorney** held by someone in Guyana, but policies differ and can change; confirm with the specific branch in writing before relying on it
- Fully remote online opening from abroad is generally **not** a thing yet

The most reliable pattern: **plan the account opening around a trip home**, with every document prepared in advance so it's one branch visit, not three.

## The document checklist

Prepare before you travel:

1. **Valid passport** — and a second photo ID if you have one
2. **TIN (Taxpayer Identification Number)** from the Guyana Revenue Authority — banks routinely require it, and you'll want it anyway for land dealings and barrel clearance
3. **Proof of address** — both your overseas address and, if applicable, your Guyana address; utility bills are the standard
4. **Proof of income / source of funds** — job letter, bank statements from your US/Canada/UK bank, pension statements. Compliance teams ask overseas applicants for more, not less
5. **A reference** — some banks ask for a reference from an existing customer or your overseas bank

Call the branch a few weeks ahead and ask for their current non-resident requirements list. Ten minutes on the phone saves a wasted morning on Camp Street.

## Managing the account from abroad

- **Online banking** exists at the major banks but is more basic than you're used to — check what the specific bank supports for international transfers and bill payments before assuming
- **Deposits from overseas** work via wire transfer (mind the [fees](/articles/cheapest-way-to-send-money-to-guyana/)) or remittance services that pay directly into Guyanese bank accounts
- **Keep the account active** — dormancy rules can freeze long-untouched accounts, which defeats the purpose
- **Local mobile wallets** like MMG+ can complement the account for bill payments

## Why bother, versus just sending transfers

A local account earns its keep when money needs to *live* in Guyana: collecting rent, paying contractors in stages while building, funding a business, holding funds for a land purchase through your attorney, or landing softly as a remigrant. If your only need is family support, regular [remittance transfers](/tools/remittance-fee-calculator/) remain simpler and often cheaper.
