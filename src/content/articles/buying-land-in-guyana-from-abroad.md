With the oil boom pushing up prices in Georgetown, on the East Bank, and along the East Coast corridor, more overseas Guyanese are buying land back home than ever. Unfortunately, the diaspora is also the number one target for land fraud. Here's how to buy safely from New York, Toronto, or London.

## Understand what you're buying: transport vs. title

Guyana's property system has two parallel regimes — a legacy of Roman-Dutch law:

- **Transported property** — ownership passes by a "transport" through the Deeds Registry, with the process advertised publicly before it completes.
- **Titled property (certificate of title)** — registered under the Land Registry system in areas it covers.

Which regime applies depends on where the land is. Your attorney handles the mechanics, but you should know which one your document is, because verifying ownership happens at different registries.

## The golden rule: verify at the registry, not by word of mouth

Every diaspora land scam starts the same way: a seller (sometimes a relative) shows convincing-looking documents for land they don't own, own only a share of, or have already sold to someone else. Before any money moves:

1. **Hire your own attorney in Guyana** — not the seller's attorney, not a family friend handling it informally.
2. **Have them run a search at the Deeds Registry or Land Registry** to confirm the seller actually owns the property and whether there are mortgages, liens, or pending transactions against it.
3. **Confirm the boundaries with a sworn land surveyor** — occupied or encroached land is common, and a fence in the wrong place is your problem after closing.
4. **Check for unpaid rates and taxes** with the local NDC or city council.

## Buying while overseas: the power of attorney

If you can't travel for the transaction, you'll execute a **power of attorney** authorizing someone in Guyana — ideally your attorney, not a casual relative — to act for you. It generally needs to be properly executed and registered in Guyana to be effective. Keep the powers **specific and limited** to the one transaction.

## Family land: the inheritance trap

A huge share of disputes involve "children's property" — land left by grandparents, never formally divided among heirs. If you're buying (or claiming) a share of family land where the estate was never administered, the sale can be challenged by other heirs years later. Estates need to be properly administered (letters of administration or probate) before anyone can validly sell.

## Red flags

- Pressure to send a deposit **before** any registry search
- A seller who can't produce the original transport or title
- Prices dramatically below the going rate for the area
- "The lawyer will handle everything" — where the lawyer works for the seller
- Requests to route money through personal accounts rather than an attorney's account

## The realistic budget

Beyond the purchase price, budget for attorney's fees, registry costs, and any applicable duties or taxes on the transfer — commonly a few percent of the property value all-in, varying with the transaction. Get the full cost breakdown from your attorney **in writing** before committing.

Bought carefully, land in Guyana has been one of the best-performing assets a diaspora family can hold. Bought carelessly, it's years of court dates on your vacation time. The difference is a registry search and a good attorney.
