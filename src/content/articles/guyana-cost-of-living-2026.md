Cost of living has become Guyana's most heated public conversation of 2026 — and in late August it boiled over, after President Irfaan Ali's explanation of *why* prices are climbing drew a wave of criticism. Here's a plain, balanced look at what was actually said, what the numbers show, and why it struck such a nerve — with sources so you can read the original reporting yourself.

## What the President said

Speaking at a news conference in August 2026, President Ali argued that Guyana's cost-of-living pressure comes from several directions at once, and that some of it reflects how spending has changed. As reported by Kaieteur News, he pointed to Guyanese increasingly **shopping in supermarkets and eating out more** than they did about four years ago, noting that supermarket prices carry higher margins — for rent and air conditioning — than the traditional market.

He also leaned heavily on factors outside the government's control:

- **Imported inflation** and a difficult global environment, including Middle East tensions affecting the price of pharmaceuticals and fertiliser
- Guyana's **dependence on imported refined fuel** — while the country now produces crude oil, it still imports gasoline, diesel, and cooking gas, so global fuel prices flow straight through to consumers

On solutions, the President was clear that he does not see handouts as the answer. In his words, "No amount of grants would address it" — arguing instead for what he called a holistic, multidimensional approach.

## What the data actually shows

Beneath the politics, the economics are fairly specific. According to the **Inter-American Development Bank's** Caribbean Economics Quarterly, headline inflation in Guyana has stayed relatively moderate — around **2.9% at the end of 2025** and **2.6% by February 2026**. But the average hides where the pain is: **food**.

- Food prices rose about **4.4% in 2025**, and roughly **5.9% by early 2026**
- Medical care (about 4.9%) and miscellaneous items (about 6.8%) also climbed in 2025

The IDB identified food as the single biggest contributor to inflation — which matches what households feel at the market even when the overall percentage looks small. When the things that rise fastest are the things you buy every day, a "low" inflation figure doesn't feel low.

## The measures on the table

The President framed the response as a combination of steps rather than one silver bullet. The most talked-about was a proposal for **permanent farmers' markets** across the country, connecting growers directly to buyers to cut out mark-ups — an idea he has floated more than once. He also cited existing actions his administration points to, including **tax removal for farmers** and various **subsidies**.

The consistent message: cash grants may cushion the blow, but the government's position is that they don't fix the underlying drivers.

## Why it sparked a backlash

The reaction was swift and largely critical. Kaieteur News and its commentators characterised the remarks as **"tone deaf"** and **out of touch**, and reported citizens pushing back hard on the suggestion that changing personal habits was part of the answer. The core objection, as covered across the outlet, was that people wanted the government to act to bring prices *down* directly — rather than being told to shop or eat differently while their grocery bills climbed.

State media framed the same remarks differently, emphasising the role of shifting consumption patterns and global pressures rather than the criticism. That gap — between how the story was told in the independent press and in state outlets — became part of the story itself.

## What it means for the diaspora

For Guyanese overseas, this debate isn't abstract — it shows up in what your family asks for. When food and fuel prices rise faster than the headline inflation rate, **remittances and barrels don't stretch as far**. Many diaspora families are quietly sending more, or shipping more goods, to cover the same needs.
